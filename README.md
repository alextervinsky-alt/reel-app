# Reel

A personal Mac app that scans film drives, recognises each film from its file name, and shows it as a good-looking library to help decide what to watch. Bonus material in a film's folder (interviews, trailers, an `Extras` folder, subtitles, notes) is kept with the film instead of showing up as films of its own. Reel never changes, moves or writes film files and never writes to film drives: scanning only reads folder listings (names, sizes, dates), and files are only opened when you press Play or open an extra.

Everything that comes from the web refreshes on its own while Reel is open (checked at launch and every six hours, each item only when due): film info and ratings monthly, award lists weekly, collecting sets every two weeks, the IMDb ratings copy and list posters monthly, Explore lists every six hours.

**Look.** Apple's own font (SF Pro) throughout, a neutral near-black background and one accent, a warm projector-light orange, kept for what's chosen or needs you. The sidebar floats as a rounded panel inset from the window's edges and starting under the window's close, minimise and full-screen buttons: Tonight shows as a card with its posters once something is on it, and the drives sit in a slim block at its foot, each on one small line with whether it's connected and its free space (read when drives come and go, after a scan and when Reel comes to the front), then what needs fixing (matches to check, films not on the Backup). There's no gray toolbar strip: the toolbar is see-through, so pictures reach the top of the window; it keeps the window buttons and Back (⌘[ too), and the sidebar hides from View › Hide Sidebar (⌃⌘S). Above the posters: the shelf's title with a line of numbers ("84 films · 78 unwatched · 6 watched · 50 new · 187 hours") and the search field, then one row of filters (Mood, Shot on, Length, Language, Clear), a slider for the posters' size, Sort and posters or a list. Each poster says Tonight, watched and NEW, and under it the title and year · running time · director; under the pointer the ratings appear on it (IMDb, Rotten Tomatoes and Metacritic) with when it would end and its subtitles. The file's quality (4K, 1080p, Dolby Vision) is left for the film's page. Scrolling stays smooth while film info is updated: the library waits to redraw until the scroll stops, and posters that arrive mid-scroll appear without fading in. The list shows every film in a row: poster, title and director, year, length, genre and mood, ratings, what it was shot on and your status; clicking Film, Year, Length or Ratings sorts it (New Arrivals, Not on Backup and search results keep their own order). A film page's tabs are pills, and its Overview has **At a Glance**: director, writers, camera, music, editor and design (each a click from their page), what it was shot on, language, the file, subtitles, where your copies are and your own status. **All Films** opens in a random order, different each time you come back to it (or by title, year, rating, date added or running time), with a search field beside its title (⌘F); the banner at the top opens its film. When Reel opens it reads the library in the background and scans every connected drive; drives connected later are scanned as they appear. Only a scan that read the whole drive while it stayed connected removes films that are gone: when a folder or file can't be read, nothing is removed (new and moved films are still added, and a notice names what couldn't be read); a scan that's stopped, or whose drive is disconnected or remounted during it, isn't used at all; a drive that reconnects or is rescanned mid-read is read again when that read ends; and a complete scan that would remove at least 10 films and a quarter of a drive's films asks first: confirming reads the drive once more, and that read removes them only if it's complete and finds no more missing. A file is known by its name and size: the same name and size is the same file (a copy on the Backup, or moved), so files that only share a generic name (title_t00.mkv) never share a match, a fix or notes; a fix saved by an older Reel under the name alone is used only when it can mean one file; otherwise files still to be looked up are matched afresh and marked Check Match (films already matched keep their match). A file replaced in place by one of another size (a new encode) keeps its fix and notes. Films found since a drive's first scan show under **New Arrivals** for 14 days, and a wishlist film that turns up in the library leaves the wishlist on its own (tagged "From your wishlist" there). A drive marked as **Backup** is compared with the main drive by film and file size, and **Not on Backup** lists what's missing or only half copied: a steady checklist by title that changes only when a drive has been read in full, saying when the Backup was last read (and that it's this reading while the Backup is disconnected). **Unwatched** has **Seen It?**: the films one poster at a time, the best known first, answered Seen or Not Seen (← →), with your stars if you remember them (1–5); a film marked there has no date, and Undo (⌘Z) takes an answer back. All Films' search finds stand-ins for words ("cartoon" finds animation, "docs" documentaries, "sci-fi" science fiction), forgives a slip (a letter wrong, missing or swapped) in a title, a name, a genre, a mood or a way of shooting only when nothing matches as typed (never a character's name: "cartoon" doesn't find a Carson), and understands the craft too ("16mm", "anamorphic", "IMAX", "black and white", "natural light", "handheld", "Steadicam", a cinematographer's name), and a **Shot on** filter sits beside Mood, Length and Language (Film, Digital, Anamorphic, IMAX, Black & white): Reel reads each film's Wikipedia article and the interviews about it in the background, two films at a time, the same texts in the same order as the Cinematography tab (and what the tab reads becomes the film's summary too), and keeps the names of the gear and techniques with the sentence behind each Shot on (pointing at Shot on on At a Glance shows it and where it was read, never a sentence about the story). A tag needs a sentence saying this film was shot that way: gear named after "not", "never", "instead of", "rather than", "unlike", "considered", "as if", "would have", "decided against", "tests of", "versus" or "compared to" in its own clause doesn't count ("shot digitally rather than on film" is digital; "tests of digital cameras versus 35mm" is neither; "their decision against anamorphic lenses" isn't anamorphic), nor does gear named for someone's work on another film ("his Super 16mm work on Carol"), in a sentence about another film (a person's film by its title, "Christopher Nolan's Oppenheimer", when that person didn't make this one; a year more than three before this film's), or for how it was finished or shown ("photochemical finishing", "70 mm prints", a restoration; "shot on 35 mm and finished photochemically" is still 35 mm). Black & white, IMAX and Anamorphic said only of part of the film ("for the flashbacks", "the battle scenes", "simulated television footage") show as "in part" ("Black & white (in part)"), unless something says it of the whole film; Film and Digital both show when a film mixes them. A Shot on tag you know is wrong can be removed for that film (right-click Shot on › Not Right): it isn't shown, filtered or searched for it, and Settings › Performance lists it to put back. and a magazine article found by searching for the title (American Cinematographer's whole site, its articles, posts and news, and British Cinematographer's posts, both most relevant first) is read only when it names the film's cinematographer (or its year, when the cinematographer isn't known) and its title names no other film of the film's collection (read from TMDB), so a remake, a sequel or a film of a similar name never lends its gear ("The Matrix Resurrections" names the Wachowskis and 1999, but not Bill Pope). Black & white comes from Wikidata's colour, or TMDB's keyword only when Wikidata says nothing (a film partly in colour isn't black and white); TMDB's IMAX keyword isn't used, as it marks IMAX releases too. A reading from fewer texts than last time (a site down) doesn't replace the last one, and is tried again in a week; Settings › Performance lists the films whose Shot on changed when read again, and how many live-action films have Shot on, how many were read from their Wikipedia article alone and how many are still to read (when finding what to read improves, only films read from too little are read again). An animated film says "Animated" under Shot on, and a film with nothing found says which sources were read; the sidebar says how many are still to read, and a search that finds nothing meanwhile says it may find more once they're read. **Recommended** offers five films per launch, with mood chips (from the moods All Films already uses, plus Short). Every time you watch a film is kept (one viewing an evening): finishing a film you've seen before ("Did you finish it?" asks again) adds a viewing, the film page says how many times and when last, changing the date there corrects the last viewing, and marking a film unwatched forgets its viewings. Your Notes still saves the last date where Reel 1.8.7 and older look for it, but changing anything in an older Reel keeps only that last date. **Year in Film** sums up what you watched each year (films, each counted once, hours counting every viewing, rewatches, and how that compares with the year before (this year so far against last year by the same day), month by month, highlights (your favourite, the longest and shortest, the oldest and newest, the first of the year), then in numbers: the average film, your average stars, new releases, your busiest month and film night, how many languages, your longest run of nights in a row, how old a film from your drives was on average when you saw it and how many you rated (films seen elsewhere, whose dates are a guess, never count for nights, runs or ages); then your stars from one to five and your film nights by day of the week; the films you liked more, and less, than most (your stars against the average score, five stars as 10, two points apart or more); then genres, decades, the directors, cinematographers and composers you came back to, the great lists your films are on, languages and the faces you saw most, each with how many films and a bar measured against the one that leads). Everything is counted from each film's own facts (its year, running time, TMDB's credits) or your own dates and stars, never from where a film was made or shot, which co-productions make uncertain; films you saw elsewhere (the cinema, streaming) can be dated, rated and counted in it or left out with one switch. A film marked seen elsewhere is dated the month after it came out (or now, for a film still in cinemas), which you can change; films marked before Reel did this can be dated by their release in one click. A wrongly grouped file can be fixed with a right-click ("This Is an Extra Of…", "This Is a Film"); the fix is kept in Your Notes and applied on every scan.

**Spoiler-safe** (on by default): until a film is marked watched, review quotes (each shown once) and **Behind the Film** keep the story, twists and ending hidden, each with "Show anyway". Behind the Film starts with **Did You Know?**: five facts, the most surprising first (what changed, who turned it down, what was improvised, built or done first; the making before its takings and prizes, one of those at most), each with what the article says next (whole sentences: never cut at "No." or "St.", and never one that starts with a "he" or "she" it can't say who is), then the film at a glance, then tells its Wikipedia article as the film's life from idea to legacy: numbered chapters in the order it lived them (the idea, casting, the shoot, design and effects, music, release, how it was received, awards, legacy), whatever order the article uses. Each opens on its best lines, someone's own words set apart and a fact or two, and opens in place to read the whole of it. *Before you watch* has the making of, context and reception; *After you watch* (the story, its themes, every paragraph that gives something away) stays closed until the film is watched. **Where It Was Filmed** lists every filming place with what it is (from Wikidata) and the article's own sentences about the locations, studios and stages. Links to Roger Ebert, Criterion, the BFI, Senses of Cinema, Letterboxd and IMDb follow. Under the title, badges show the lists a film is on (Palme d'Or, Sight & Sound, Criterion, IMDb Top 250). **Tonight** is a shortlist for the evening, compared side by side in aligned rows (year and genres, director and leads, ratings, verdict, why it's offered, premise, end time, moods, subtitles and quality, the lists it's on), cleared at 6 in the morning; a row none of the films has anything for is left out. Recommended learns from your shared star ratings and says why each film is there in one short line (a film you loved by the same director, the watchlist, new on a drive, a list it's on, or the best in a mood); "Not tonight" keeps a film out for two weeks. Marking a film watched asks "How was it?". Films open in IINA, VLC or the default app; a film you have in two versions asks which to play.

**Cinema mode** (⇧⌘C, the TV button in the sidebar, the View menu or Settings) turns Reel into a 10-foot view for the TV: full screen and edge to edge, with big targets meant for a phone used as a mouse and a Back button always at the top. It keeps only what helps decide, in text of 22 points or more. Three tabs: **For You** (at most 15 rows in a fixed order: Tonight, Recommended, New Arrivals, Like the Films You Loved, Watchlist, up to six genres, up to three languages with enough unwatched films, Under 100 Minutes, Acclaimed and Watch Again; within each row films of about the same rating are shuffled once per evening), **Tonight** (the shortlist in aligned columns with **Decide for Us**) and **All Films** (one row of filters, sort, mood, length, language, what it was shot on and unwatched, each opening a large panel, and a search field; Random is the default sort). A poster at rest shows its title and year · running time; under the pointer or the arrow keys its ratings take the second line, and the poster shows when it would end, a moon for Tonight and a trailer bar. The arrow keys reach everything: the tabs and Exit (up from the top of a page), the mood chips, the search field, the filters and the choices in their panels, a film page's buttons and the rows under it; the highlight is a white ring (posters scrolling under a resting pointer never take it). Return opens a film (and plays it on its page), Space plays the trailer, T adds to or takes off Tonight, Esc goes back. A film's page fits one screen and says HDR or SDR beside its year, with Reviews (the critics' consensus, what people like and don't like, awards), **Did You Know?** (three short facts, large, spoiler-safe), From the Same People and More Like This below. Back from the player, "Did you finish it?" and "How was it?" come up TV-sized, with big stars picked with the arrow keys. Turning Cinema mode off returns the window and the library exactly as they were.

Films open full screen in IINA or VLC (Settings › Watching › Play full screen, always in Cinema mode; VLC is asked through AppleScript, which macOS confirms once, and with VLC as the player it's started out of sight when a film page, Recommended or Cinema mode opens, so Play doesn't wait for it to load; Reel 1.7.1 switched a VLC setting to IINA once, for HDR on the TV), and coming back to Reel after watching long enough asks "Did you finish it?", then "How was it?". Under a film's title a tag says whether it's HDR (Dolby Vision, HDR10+, HDR10, HLG) or SDR, so the TV can be set before Play, for the copy Play opens (other copies only when they differ), with IMAX or Open Matte beside it when the file name says so (also on At a Glance's File line). Reel never guesses SDR: it says SDR only when something proves it, and pointing at the tag says what: your own setting (the film's "…" menu › Picture: Dolby Vision, HDR or SDR, kept in Your Notes for that file, and not passed to a new encode of it), the file name ("DV HDR10", "SDR"), a source that can't carry HDR (a DVD, 720p or lower, a 1080p Blu-ray; "HDRip" is SDR), or the film's own folder ("Dune (2021) 2160p DV/dune.mkv"), or the folder of a collection named as one ("The Fast and The Furious - Complete Collection (2001-2021) 1080p HEVC HDR10": "Collection", "Trilogy", "Saga", "Box Set" or a span of years), whose name speaks for every film in it; a folder of assorted films says nothing. When nothing proves it, the kind of release gives practical guidance, said as a guess with its reason: "Likely HDR10" for a 4K Blu-ray (4K discs are HDR10 as standard), "Likely SDR" for a 4K web release or a 1080p file whose name doesn't say HDR (release names usually say it when it is). Only a file with nothing to go on (renamed by hand) shows a grey "HDR or SDR not stated". Search finds films by their picture ("HDR", "Dolby Vision", "SDR"), and Settings › Performance counts the files by how their picture is known. The file itself is never opened. (Reel 1.8.8 and older don't know your Picture settings, and 1.9.0 and older the Shot on tags you removed: Your Notes saved by one of them drops them.) A film's page has five tabs: Overview, Reviews, Behind the Film, Cinematography and Extras (file details are in the "…" menu). **Reviews** sums up what people like and don't like from TMDB's user reviews and the critics Wikipedia quotes: each part of a sentence is read on its own ("praised the visuals but faulted the pacing"), a critic counts by the first thing they're said to have done (praised, faulted; "not impressed" turns it around), "score" means the music only when it isn't a rating, and sentences of figures (Metacritic, box office) are left out, as are rankings, polls, prizes and nominations that don't say what anyone liked. A dislike has to be said in words: a complaint, a critic said to have faulted something, or a viewer who rated the film low; a sentence's tone alone never makes one ("a disquieting chiller" is praise). It looks for two likes and two dislikes: a viewer's complaint (in whole words: "unpredictable" isn't "predictable") counts even in a warm review ("my only complaint is that it drags", "a bit confusing at times", the aspect read from the complaint itself), and so does a real complaint about something most people liked, with its own sentence; when nobody complained about anything, nothing is made up. **Cinematography** is told the way American Cinematographer tells a film. It reads the film's Wikipedia article, the interviews and craft articles it cites (American Cinematographer, British Cinematographer, Kodak, ARRI, No Film School, IndieWire's craft pieces…; never an interview with the editor or composer, never the interviewer's questions), American Cinematographer's and British Cinematographer's own articles about the film, Kodak's and No Film School's stories of it, the cinematographer's Wikipedia article and Wikidata's awards, then shows: who shot it (their page a click away), who they are, how they came to work with the director and the awards the cinematography won or was up for; frames from the film; the look in brief and a specification sheet (camera, lenses, film and format, aspect ratio, frame rate, colour, lighting, grip and movement, filters, lab and finish; formats only when the sentence is about the shooting, not the release); then **What to Look For**: the five most telling notes, numbered (the visual idea, their own words, what it drew on, a particular scene, a challenge, the frame, the light, the colour; at most two of a kind), ranked by the gear and precise detail they name, their reason, the filmmakers' own account and how much they surprise, with everything else read by topic under More on How It Was Shot. Every note opens to the paragraph it comes from (the note picked out in it) and what the gear and techniques it names are, and every value on the specification sheet opens to what it is and what it does to the picture (camera, lenses, film and format, frame, light, rigs, filters and finish), each with where it was read and the gear in bold; any name in a note Reel can explain (the gear, and ways of shooting such as a long take, a dolly zoom, magic hour or chiaroscuro, from a glossary of about 150) is a link that shows what it is right under the sentence; a sentence that only says who shot it is left out, and so is anything from what critics or awards said, the music, release and reception sections, or the story before the film is watched. Then the cinematographer's other films in your library, in the order they were made, the camera, lighting and colour crew from TMDB, the interviews to read in full, and links to IMDb's technical specifications, ShotOnWhat and American Cinematographer. A cinematographer's page shows what they're known for and their recurring gear and looks across their films in your library. Overview has **Pair It With**: one film from your library for a double feature, with why (two by the same director, the same cinematographer, the same writer or lead, the same way of shooting, a rare shared theme that gives nothing of either story away), then **From the Same People** (director, writer, cinematographer, composer, then the two leads, up to 12 films) and **More Like This**, which ranks films in your library by what viewers of the film went on to like (TMDB recommendations), rare shared themes, tone and genre, and keeps family films and documentaries apart from adult films, and gentle films (animated, family, feel-good) apart from violent or bleak ones however many themes they share. **Trailers** are the film's own full trailers on YouTube (a teaser only when there's no trailer), in English, with no language or in the film's language, HD uploads first (YouTube then plays them as sharp as the player's size allows), the studio's uploads first and others' (an archive's) only as a fallback; TMDB's label isn't trusted alone, so a video must call itself a trailer or teaser (a studio's upload named only for the film counts when labelled a trailer), and one named a TV spot, clip, scene, featurette, character piece ("Meet the Pack"), announcement, reaction or fan edit is never played; they play inside Reel in YouTube's privacy-enhanced player over the film's backdrop, nothing kept on disk, with Reel's own controls: click the timeline to jump anywhere (or drag), click the picture or press Space to pause, ← and → for ten seconds back or on. Full Screen (or F) fills the screen with the player, and a trailer started from a preview plays in the window's own player. Up to five are tried in turn; when one ends, the film's backdrop and Play Again cover YouTube's suggestions; when none plays the button disappears, and only the library offers "Watch on YouTube". Cinema mode never opens a browser, and if three films in a row fail it hides trailers until Reel is opened again. In the library, Space on the poster under the pointer (or right-click › Quick Look) shows a quick preview (a click outside it or Esc closes it, as with a preview of a film you don't own), and the moon on a poster under the pointer adds it to Tonight; All Films filters by mood chips, what it was shot on, length and language. Settings › Performance lists how long opening, scanning and updating took (with hundreds of films the library is brought up to date every two seconds while film info is looked up, and big refreshes are spread out); Settings scrolls at a height that fits a laptop.

**For You** (under Discover) is fifteen films you don't have and haven't seen that people who loved your favourites (4 or 5 stars, or a heart) went on to love, kept only when they're truly alike (the same kind of film, from about the same time, sharing people, themes or tone), laid out like Recommended (its frame and number, why it's there, ratings and the whole synopsis, with the trailer, details, wishlist, Seen It? and Not for Me, which keeps a film out of For You and Explore for good; Settings › Watching can suggest them again); fifteen others each time Reel opens (and New Picks draws again). Chips under the moods narrow it to the films that follow one of yours (Because you loved Parasite). **Explore** has a search for any film (those in your library first), what's in cinemas, Coming Soon (opening in the next three months, each with its date), trending and new at home, shelves that change each time Reel opens (Because You Loved one of your films, More from the director of one, only the films they directed that you don't have, a country's cinema, a decade or the hidden gems; a film's thumbs-down under the pointer leaves it out for good), and the best of any year from the source you choose (TMDB or IMDb rating, most popular, award winners, hidden gems), the source and the year each picked in one click from a short list. Films you've seen are left out, and rows that rank by quality are reordered each launch within films of about the same rating. Mood chips narrow every row to one mood and add that mood's best-loved films from any time. The Lists hub starts with a search for lists by any words (a theme like heist or lighthouse, a series, a person, a country's cinema or a decade, and Wikipedia's "List of … films"), then **Start Here**: the films the critics' polls, festivals and rankings agree on that you haven't seen, those already on your drives and those worth finding, ranked by how many (and which) lists they're on, how they fit your ratings and how they're rated, each saying why (a director whose film you loved, or its lists). **Your Progress** follows: the lists you're furthest through (two films seen or more), with how many you've seen and how many of the rest are already on your drives. Then IMDb's Top 100 for any year and Top 250 (from IMDb's free non-commercial ratings files), award winners from Wikidata (Oscars for Best Picture, Director, Cinematography, International and Animated Film, Cannes, Venice, Berlin, Locarno, BAFTA, César, European Film Awards), Sight & Sound and the Criterion Collection, each showing what you own and have seen, sortable by Best for You, and saying which other lists a film is on; and "Complete the Set" for directors, cinematographers and franchises. Marking a film seen asks when and how it was.

## Code

- `Sources/ReelCore`: logic with no UI: file-name reader, TMDB client, matcher, drive scanner (with film/extras grouping in `Extras.swift` and hand fixes in `GroupingFixes.swift`), new arrivals, wishlist cleanup and the Backup check (`Arrivals.swift`), recommendations (`Recommender.swift`), Year in Film, IMDb ratings files, Wikidata lists, film sets, library, storage
- `Sources/Reel/Model`: app state (`AppModel`, split by topic into `AppModel+….swift`), notes observed per film (`PersonalStore`), drive locating by volume ID, image cache
- `Sources/Reel/Views`: sidebar, grid, film page, match fixer, welcome, settings; `Views/Cinema` is Cinema mode, a separate view tree, so switching never disturbs the desk views
- `Sources/Reel/Diagnostics/ScreenRenderer.swift`: draws Cinema mode's screens to PNG at laptop, 1080p and 4K sizes (CI only, from a temporary sample library)
- `Tests/ReelCoreTests`: run on every push; `LiveTMDBTests` matches the sample films against the real TMDB
- `scripts/make-app.sh`: builds `Reel.app` (Apple Silicon, macOS 15+), signs with a certificate only if the `SIGNING_CERT_P12` secret is ever added

## Where Reel keeps things

Everything is in `~/Documents/Reel/`:

| | |
|---|---|
| `Your Notes.json` | watched, favorites, watchlist, ratings, notes, wishlist, match and grouping fixes (irreplaceable; set aside, never overwritten, if unreadable) |
| `Settings.json` | TMDB token, OMDb key, drives and which one is the Backup, the films you marked Not for Me (mode 600) |
| `Library.json` | film info per file: a pure cache, rebuilt by a rescan |
| `Safety Copies/` | one copy of Your Notes per day, last 30 days |
| `Image Cache.nosync/` | posters and photos: 400 MB cap (least recently used removed first), unused images removed after a week, excluded from iCloud and Time Machine |
| `List Data.nosync/` | IMDb ratings copy, award lists, list posters and sets (downloaded again if deleted) |

Writes are atomic, batched (notes 0.5 s, library 2 s) and run on a background queue; everything is flushed on quit. Network requests use no disk cache and no cookies. Window state restoration is off. Nothing is ever written to film drives.

## Builds

GitHub Actions builds on every push with `[build]` in the commit message, or by hand. Download `Reel-mac` from the run; the screens (Cinema mode at the laptop's size, 1080p and 4K, plus a few library views) are pushed to the `screens` branch.

Builds carry no keys. The TMDB token and the OMDb key (for IMDb, Rotten Tomatoes and Metacritic ratings, free from omdbapi.com) are pasted once in Reel and kept only in `Settings.json`; CI reads its own copies from the repository's secrets (`TMDB_TOKEN`, `OMDB_KEY`) for the live tests and the screens (TMDB only), and never prints them. The file names in the tests are made up.
